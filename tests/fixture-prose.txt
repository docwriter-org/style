It is in his moments of movement that Mallarme is exciting, and his
moments of lingering are his moments of extreme conventionality. He
has no hold unless he is in motion. When he pauses he is not
impressive; and he pauses often, and is then content with a
convention. The value of his work is the value of a journey; it is
the way, and not the arriving. He is a poet of travelling, and not
of staying. The staying is a concession, and a slight one.

There is a splendid and lonely courage in the rejection of the
accustomed word when the accustomed word is not the inevitable word.
He will not take the phrase that lies in wait. He will not have the
easy metaphor, the ready epithet, the expected cadence. The
language he wants is the language that has not yet been used for
that thought, and he will wait, or he will invent, rather than
accept a substitute that has served another man.

This is why his poems look, at a first glance, like a refusal. They
refuse the reader's memory. They refuse the comfort of a known
tune. They refuse, almost angrily, the compliment of being like
something else. And yet the refusal is not a trick and not a
display. It is the only way he can keep the thought from settling
into a phrase that already belongs to the world.

He is not obscure because he loves darkness. He is obscure because
he will not light a borrowed lamp. The reader who wants a familiar
room will call that darkness. The reader who will walk with him
finds that the path is lit by the thing itself — by the exact noun,
the unrepeatable adjective, the verb that has not been worn smooth.

There is also a tenderness in this severity. He does not punish the
reader. He asks the reader to come a little further than habit
usually goes. That is a form of respect. It assumes that the reader
can take a new word without being led by the hand, and can prefer a
precise difficulty to a soft and inaccurate ease.
